Abstract:

The model for structure-function relationships, hemoglobin (Hb), embodies the prime
functional importance of the structure of a biochemically active molecule. The major
oxygen transport metalloprotein of the vertebrate erythrocyte, physiologically this
function of the molecule is dependent upon the molecule‘s complex four level
structure. In this case study the structural hierarchy of hemoglobin, biochemical
functioning of the heme prosthetic group in oxygen transport, the allosteric
conformational changes governing cooperativity and mutation in hemoglobin,
focusing on the pathology of Sickle Cell Anaemia is studied.

1. Biological Function and Physiological Role
Hemoglobin sits at the interface of the respiratory and circulatory systems and is
responsible for oxygen (O2) loading in the pulmonary capillaries, where partial
pressure of oxygen (pO2) is high and unloading in the peripheral tissues where (pO2)
is low.
Beyond oxygen delivery, hemoglobin is vital for:
 Carbon Dioxide Transport: Facilitating the return of CO2 from tissues to the
lungs (carrying approximately 15-20% of the blood's CO2 as
carbaminohemoglobin).
 Proton Buffering: Acting as a crucial pH buffer in the blood by binding H+ ions,
a phenomenon intimately linked to the Bohr effect, which facilitates further
oxygen unloading in actively metabolizing tissues.

2. The Structural Hierarchy of Hemoglobin
 Primary Structure: Adult Hb A, human, has four different polypeptide chains, two
identical alpha (α) chains, each of 141 amino acids, and two identical beta (β)
chains of 146 amino acids.
 Secondary Structure: The individual polypeptide chains are largely populated by
a large amount of α helix. About 75% of the sequence of each chain is in the form
of eight α helices linked by very short non-helical loop regions.
 Tertiary Structure: The 3D structure of each chain is essentially globular and
mostly spherical. The structure of the fold of these globular proteins is primarily
determined by the hydrophobic effect, which results in the non-polar amino acid
side chains in the interior of the molecule whereas the hydrophilic polar amino
acid side chains located on the exterior of the molecule, in contact with the
interstitial aqueous cellular environment. This fold is essential in creating a cleft
or pocket to accommodate the prosthetic group.
 Quaternary structure: The active hemoglobin molecule is a heterotetramer (α2β2).
The four subunits are attached by many non covalent bonds, of these hydrogen
bonds and salt bridges (ionic interactions) are common. The relative interactions
between unlike subunits (e.g: α1β1 and α2β2) are very strong and make up the
tightly stable dimers.

3. The Heme Prosthetic Group: The Functional Core
The polypeptides, by themselves, have no oxygen binding ability. The binding affinity
is contained solely within the heme group, which is a firmly attached, non-protein
prosthetic group inside the hydrophobic pocket of each subunit.
 Chemical Composition: The prosthetic group of Heme is a complex organic ring
structure called protoporphyrin IX which is attached to a centrally located ferrous
iron molecule, (Fe2+).
 Coordinate Bonds: The Fe2+ at the centre can form up to six coordinate bonds. 4
of these bonds are with the nitrogen atoms in the flat porphyrin ring.
 The Fifth and Sixth sites: The fifth site of coordination is the nitrogen atom of
one of two particular residues of histidine (called the proximal histidine) on the F
helix of the protein chain. The sixth site of coordination, which is temporarily left
empty, is the given binding site for molecular oxygen, O2.
 The Protective Environment: Unprotected free heme in solution is quickly
oxidized from Fe 2+ (ferrous) to Fe 3+ (ferric) by water, forming methemoglobin
which does not bind oxygen. The hydrophobic pocket given by the tertiary
structure shields heme from water, preventing this happening and allowing
reversible oxygen binding. Another histidine (distal histidine) lies close to the
sixth coordination site, stabilizing the oxygen molecule bound by hydrogen bonds,
thus preventing the carbon monoxide from binding too tightly.

4. Allosteric Regulation and Cooperativity
Hemoglobin is an allosteric protein, this means that the function at one site is affected
by the binding of a molecule at a separate site. This ability to change shape is the key
to its efficiency.
Hemoglobin exists in two primary conformational states:
 The T-State (Tense): This is the form of deoxygenated hemoglobin. The tetramer
is stabilized rigidly by the salt bridges between the subunits. In this state, the
plane of the porphyrin ring is displaced in comparison to the oxygenated form in
which the ion is covalently linked to the porphyrin ring. The binding site for
oxygen is therefore sterically hindered. The T-state has a low affinity for oxygen.
 The R-State (Relaxed): This is the oxygenated state. When the oxygen binds it
physically breaks the stabilizing salt bridges so the subunits are free to rotate and
slide past one another. The R-state has a great affinity to oxygen.
The Mechanism of Cooperativity:
 In the T-state when the first molecule of is bound to a heme group it pulls the
down into the plane of the porphyrin ring. As a result of the covalent linkage to
the proximal histidine this causes a movement of, in effect, the entire F-helix.
 This micro-conformation change makes the salt bridges that keep the tetramer in
the T state become over stretched and break. The rest of the protein-macroscopical
change takes place in the transition to the R state. The binding sites for the other
three unoxygenated heme groups open, greatly attracting them to the oxygen.
 This structure based cooperativity is what causes hemoglobin‘s oxygen
dissociation curve to be sigmoidal (S curve) showing a greater efficiency of
combining with oxygen in the lungs and releasing it into tissues more effectively
than other simple non-cooperative proteins such as myoglobin.

5.Clinical Correlation: Sickle Cell Anemia
The fact that the physiological function of hemoglobin is exquisitely sensitive to form
are demonstrated most by the disorder Sickle Cell Anemia (SCA). SCA is an
autosomal recessive disease caused by a mutation at 1 residue in the gene encoding
the β-chain of hemoglobin.
The Molecular Defect:
 In normal hemoglobin (HbA), the 6th amino acid along the primary sequence of
the β-chain is Glutamic acid (Glu). Glutamic acid is a negatively charged and
hydrophilic amino acid that is located on the outside surface of the folded protein,
thus interacting with the aqueous environment of the red blood cell cytoplasm.
 In Sickle Cell Hemoglobin (Hb S) this mutation is one where this residue is
replaced with a Valine (Val). This residue of amino acid, Valine, is a neutral
and highly hydrophobic compound.

The Structural Consequence:
This hydrophobic valine that exists on the outside surface of the protein is
thermodynamically unstable in an aqueous environment. When HbS exists in its
oxygenated R-state, this valine site is partially occluded. Conversely, when HbS
oxygen dissociates into the deoxygenated T-state, a complementary hydrophobic
cavity is created on the surface of a neighboring of β-chain.
In order to leave the aqueous environment, the mutated Valine of a single tetramer of
HbS binds accurately into the hydrophobic pocket of a different tetramer of HbS.

Pathological Polymerization:
This abnormal hydrophobic interaction in HbS tetramers results in ternary
polymerization to n long, high viscosity, insoluble fibers (tactoids) in the red cell.
The stiff polymorphic fibers physically deform the RBC, bending the normal resilient
biconcave disc into a permanent stiff crescent or “sickle” shape. These abnormal
sickled RBCs rapidly lose their normal rheological flexibility and become lodged
within the small blood vessels. The consequences of this are vaso occlusion,
localized ischemia, painful ischemic episodes and early destruction of the RBCs
(haemolysis), resulting in anemia and progressively causing organ damage.
